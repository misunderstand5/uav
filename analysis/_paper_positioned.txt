I. INTRODUCTION
A. Background and Motivation
Unmanned aerial vehicles Acan be rapidly deployed
in emergency and hotspot scenarios, such as disaster relief
and service restoration, and their three-dimensional mobility
enables line-of-sight-dominated air-to-ground links. A single
UAV is constrained by limited onboard energy and radio
resources, restricting its coverage and capacity to serve con-
current users. Cooperative multi-UAV systems can alleviate
these limitations by extending coverage and distributing traf
loads, but they also introduce cochannel interference, inter-
UAV resource contention, and coupled trajectory and resource-
aerial backhaul from the ground-user access, so that the two
tiers can be controlled separately but in coordination. Its

delivered over both tiers rather than by the coverage geometry

Such missions are oftendynamic and high-load: the number
of active ground users varies within a mission, and
during peaks their aggregate minimum-rate demand exceeds
the capacity of the access and backhaul links. Random user
arrivals and departures change the active user population,
while mobility alters the spatial distribution of demand and
wireless link conditions. Consequently, whether the access
link or the backhaul link limits each GU's delivered rate
changes continually. Under such loads, aggregate throughput
or energy efy can remain favorable even when some
GUs repeatedly fail to receive their minimum rates. Moreover,
a scheduler that maximizes the number of GUs in
each slot tends to favor GUs with strong channels or low
rate requirements, keeping the remaining GUs under-served
for long periods. This paper therefore aims to maximize the
fraction of GUs meeting their minimum rates while bounding
how long any GU can remain below its minimum rate.
B. A Brief Introduction of Our Work and Challenges
In this work, we study minimum-rate service in a dynamic
high-load two-tier UAV downlink, where a central UAV
UAprovides wireless backhaul to multiple marginal UAVs
Awhich in turn serve the GUs. Fig. 1 illustrates the
network architecture and operating process, and Section III
details the mathematical model.
, GUs randomly arrive, depart, and move dur-
ing the mission, and each GU keeps a ed minimum-rate
requirement for its session, so the aggregate load grows and
shrinks with the number and service classes of active GUs. The
C-UAV forwards data to the M-UAVs over a power-domain
non-orthogonal multiple access backhaul, and each
M-UAV shares its access resources among its associated GUs.
A GU is in a slot only if its end-to-end delivered
rate reaches its minimum rate, and the outcomes of successive
slots form each GU's history of consecutive under-service. Our
objective is to maximize the time-averaged fraction of active
GUs that meet their minimum rates in each slot, while limiting
the longest consecutive under-service of each GU.
Solving this problem raises three main challenges.
Dynamic khaul coupling: Strong access links
alone do not guarantee delivery when the serving relay is
backhaul-limited. Conversely, suf backhaul does not

overcome poor access efy. Session churn and mobility
change both bottlenecks, while C-UAV trajectory and power
decisions redistribute downlink backhaul capacity. In the con-
sidered system, M-UAVs reuse a common access resource
while the C-UAV distributes NOMA backhaul power
across relays. Thus, improving the wireless backhaul of one
relay need not resolve access scarcity in another. The controller
must therefore account for both the geometry-dependent rates
and the resource competition in end-to-end delivery.
Completion versus persistent under-service:When demand
exceeds capacity, spreading partial rates over all users com-
pletes few of them. Serving the users with the lowest com-
pletion cost maximizes the number of sessions in
each slot, but repeating this choice starves the same resource-
expensive sessions slot after slot. Mean completion and the
tail of consecutive under-service therefore and the
scheduler must balance them explicitly rather than optimize
either one alone.
Heterogeneous action credit and relay structure:C-UAV
motion reshapes the network geometry and affects service
over many subsequent slots, whereas backhaul power mainly
redistributes capacity within the current slot. A single value
target therefore misassigns credit to at least one of the two
actions. In addition, M-UAVs are interchangeable service
entities whose index order carries no physical meaning and
whose number may vary, so the controller requires a relay
representation that respects this structure.
To address these challenges, we proposehistory-aware QoS-
completion soft actor a framework that
couples a slot-level history-aware QoS-completion scheduler
with a learned branch-credit C-UAV controller.
The scheduler determines feasible minimum-rate completions
under two-hop resource constraints, while the learned con-
troller adapts C-UAV motion and backhaul-power allocation.
The two components interact through the delivered service:
the controller sets the capacities that the scheduler allocates,
and the scheduling outcomes form the controller's reward and
next observation.
The main contributions, each addressing the corresponding
challenge, are summarized as follows.
Dynamic High-Load Two-Tier UAV Communication Mod-
eling and Problem Formulation:We establish a dynamic
two-tier UAV downlink model with time-varying GU pop-
ulations and ed per-session minimum-rate requirements.
By jointly accounting for shared access resources and
power-domain NOMA backhaul constraints, we character-
ize the end-to-end minimum-QoS completion conditions
under limited two-hop capacity. We then formulate a ser-
vice optimization problem that considers both minimum-
QoS completion efy and consecutive under-service
in dynamic high-load conditions.
History-Aware Two-Hop Completion Scheduling:We de-
velop HA-QCS, which screens the users whose access
links can support their minimum rates, then rescues the most
overdue of them, completes the others in ascending order
of two-hop resource cost, and distributes the residual
resources. A single guard threshold on the consecutive
under-service duration thus controls the tail targeted by the
continuity constraint. With 120 users, HA-QCS shortens the
95th-percentile outage by more than sevenfold relative to
scheduling at a small cost in completion.
Branch-Credit C-UAV Control with Relay-Structured Repre-
sentation:We develop a branch-credit learning framework
for joint C-UAV motion and backhaul-power control, in
which the two decision branches are trained with objec-
tives their distinct temporal effects on end-to-end
service., the motion branch employs a recursive
soft value objective to capture long-term service outcomes,
whereas the power branch uses a same-slot service utility
to address immediate backhaul allocation. A shared relay-
structured encoder with masked pooling supports a variable
number of interchangeable M-UAVs.

VI. DISCUSSION ANDLIMITATIONS
A. Insights on the Trade-off
The results show that mean completion and the tail of
consecutive under-service must be treated as separate targets.
Cost-ordered completion maximizes the former but repeatedly
skips the same expensive sessions, whereas a small, guarded
deviation from the cost order removes most of the tail at
little cost in completion. The rescue, however, is not an
outage deadline: a GU withC A
i;n < R min
i cannot be rescued
by reordering, and recovering it requires C-UAV motion, a
different association, or more backhaul power, which is where
the learned controller contributes.
B. Limitations and Future Work
Several limits qualify these First, the access model
uses orthogonal access-resource fractions and mean inter-
relay interference; time-frequency scheduling and
instantaneous fading are beyond this model. Second, access
and backhaul use independent logical resource budgets without
an explicit cross-tier duplex or inter-tier interference mech-
anism. Third, the simulated metric is a per-slot minimum-
rate threshold; it is not equivalent to a 3GPP time-window
GFBR or an end-to-end packet deadline guarantee. Fourth,
HA-QCS selects users greedily with a ed rescue quota
of one GU per relay; under very high load, many sessions
become overdue at once and this quota limits how far the guard
threshold can shorten the tail; in the ed-population study, the
continuity constraint withTout = 15 s is therefore violated for
N 80, and an adaptive quota is a natural extension. HA-
QCS also returns no allocation at a relay with no individually
feasible GU, a choice that a best-effort fallback could relax.
Fifth, the size is ed within each experiment. Finally,
the are based on stochastic simulation without
measurements, and the intervals scenario
rather than retraining variability.
Natural extensions are to model radio-block occupancy
and cross-tier coexistence explicitly, to let service history
also guide user association and M-UAV mobility, and to
evaluate time-window QoS contracts under realistic mobility
and energy dynamics.
